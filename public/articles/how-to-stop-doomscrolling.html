To stop doomscrolling, make the feed harder to open, give your hands and mind something else to do at your usual trigger moments, and set clear limits on when you check news and social apps. Willpower alone rarely works, because feeds are designed to have no natural stopping point. Changing your environment works better than trying to resist in the moment.

What is doomscrolling?

Doomscrolling is the habit of endlessly scrolling through negative or alarming content, usually news or social media, even though it makes you feel worse. Because new posts keep loading, there is never a clear end, so you can lose a lot of time without deciding to.

Step 1: Notice your triggers

For a few days, pay attention to when you pick up your phone without a reason. Common triggers are waking up, waiting in line, boredom at your desk, stress after work and lying in bed at night. Write them down. You cannot change a habit you have not seen.

Step 2: Move the apps out of reach

Take the apps you scroll most off your home screen, or into a folder on the last page. Log out after each use. Each extra step creates a pause, and that pause is often enough to ask yourself whether you really want to open the app.

Step 3: Block the feeds during your danger hours

Pick the times you scroll most and block those apps during them. Common windows are the first hour after waking, work or study hours, and the hour before bed. Using a blocker is more reliable than relying on mood, because the decision is made in advance when you are calm.

Step 4: Set a daily time limit

Choose a realistic daily cap for each app, then lower it gradually. If you currently spend three hours, a sudden limit of ten minutes will not hold. Reduce in steps and keep the limit strict enough that you notice when you reach it.

Step 5: Replace the scroll with a better default

If you remove the scroll and leave a gap, it will come back. Decide what fills the gap:

  • A book or e-reader at the bedside instead of your phone.
  • Stretching or a short walk at the afternoon slump.
  • A puzzle, a call to a friend or a sketchbook while waiting.

Step 6: Charge your phone outside the bedroom

Late-night scrolling is one of the hardest patterns to break. Buy a cheap alarm clock, plug your phone in across the room or in another room, and the bed stops being a scrolling spot.

Step 7: Choose when you read the news

Staying informed is fine. Doing it continuously is the problem. Pick one or two set times a day, such as a short check at lunch, and read from a source you trust rather than an endless feed. When the time is up, close it.

Step 8: Clean up what you follow

Mute or unfollow accounts that leave you anxious or angry. Turn off non-essential notifications. Every alert is an invitation to open the app, and most of them are not urgent.

Step 9: Track how you feel, not just the minutes

Screen time numbers matter, but so does how you feel afterwards. After a scrolling session, ask yourself if you feel better or worse. That honest answer is a good reason to keep going with the changes.

Practical examples

The morning scroll

Charge your phone outside the bedroom, use a regular alarm, and block social and news apps until you have finished a short morning routine. By the time the apps unlock, the urge is weaker.

The work-hours slip

Create a work schedule that blocks the apps you reach for when a task gets hard. When you feel the pull, the block screen gives you a few seconds to choose a different action.

The late-night loop

Set a block for the hour before bed. Replace it with a fixed wind-down routine: dim the lights, read a few pages, set tomorrow's top task.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Going cold turkey on everything. Extreme rules tend to collapse. Start with your worst app and time of day.
  • Deleting the app and reinstalling it an hour later. Blocks and limits that are hard to undo work better than a deletion you can reverse in seconds.
  • Not replacing the habit. The boredom or stress that triggered the scroll is still there.
  • Treating a slip as failure. One bad evening is data, not a verdict. Adjust the limit and carry on.
  • Keeping notifications on. They pull you back in all day.

Frequently asked questions

Why is it so hard to stop scrolling?

Feeds are endless and unpredictable, so there is always a chance the next post will be interesting. There is also no natural end point. Making the apps harder to reach helps more than trying harder.

Is doomscrolling bad for my mental health?

Many people find it leaves them more stressed or low. If scrolling is linked to persistent anxiety or low mood, consider talking to a health professional. This article is general advice, not medical advice.

Should I delete social media completely?

Not necessarily. Many people do better with limits and schedules than with a total ban. If an app causes more harm than good, deleting it is a valid choice.

How can I stop scrolling in bed?

Charge your phone away from the bed, block distracting apps for the hour before sleep, and replace the habit with reading or a short wind-down routine.
